# Module 1 --- Foundations of AI Ethics

## Basic Ethics Concepts

### Ethics

-   The study of what is right and wrong, what matters, and how people
    justify decisions.
-   Ethics is **not** simply a fixed set of rules---it helps us think
    critically about moral choices.

### Normative Ethics

-   Asks: **"What should we do?"**
-   Develops principles that guide ethical behaviour.

### Applied Ethics

-   Applies ethical principles to real-world fields such as AI,
    medicine, law, journalism, and business.

### Meta-Ethics

-   Explores what moral claims mean and whether values are universal or
    context-dependent.

## Major Ethical Theories

### Utilitarianism

-   Judges actions by their consequences.
-   Goal: maximize overall welfare.
-   **AI Example:** Deploy an AI system if its overall benefits outweigh
    its harms.

### Deontology

-   Focuses on duties, rights, and moral rules rather than outcomes.
-   **AI Example:** Privacy, dignity, and non-discrimination should
    never be violated.

### Virtue Ethics

-   Focuses on developing good character.
-   **AI Example:** Build AI with honesty, fairness, and responsibility.

## Ethical Traditions

### Ubuntu

> "A person is a person through other persons."

-   Emphasizes community well-being and collective impact.

### Communitarian Ethics

-   Places the community at the center of moral concern.

### Confucian Role Ethics

-   Moral character develops through relationships and responsibility.

### Buddhist Ethics

-   Emphasizes non-harm, compassion, and interdependence.

### Indian Dharmic Traditions

-   Ethical duty depends on role, context, and time.

## Key AI Ethics Concepts

### Ethical Pluralism

Different ethical theories can produce different but valid conclusions.

### Power Asymmetry

AI systems often know much more about users than users know about them.

### Five-Question Framework

1.  Who benefits?
2.  Who is harmed?
3.  Who decides?
4.  Who can challenge the decision?
5.  At what scale?

## Recommendation Systems

### Recommender System

Predicts content, products, or people users are likely to engage with.

### Collaborative Filtering

Recommends items liked by similar users.

### Content-Based Filtering

Recommends items similar to what a user previously liked.

### Objective Function

The metric an AI optimizes (clicks, watch time, engagement, retention).

## Risks

### Filter Bubble

Algorithms increasingly show users similar information.

### Echo Chamber

People mostly encounter opinions that reinforce existing beliefs.

### Hallucination

AI confidently generates incorrect or fabricated information.

### Parasocial Dependency

Users form emotional attachments to AI systems.

### Algorithmic Bias

AI reproduces historical unfairness present in training data.

## Responsible AI

### Transparency

Being open about how AI works and its limitations.

### Explainability

AI decisions can be understood by humans.

### Accountability

Someone can be held responsible for AI decisions.

### Non-Maleficence

**Do no harm.**

### Human Oversight

Humans can monitor and intervene in AI decisions.

### Ethics by Design

Build ethics into AI from the beginning.

### Ethics in Use

Continuously monitor and improve deployed AI.

### Governance Gates

Formal checkpoints to review or stop AI deployment.

### Redress

Users can appeal or correct AI decisions.

### Conscience by Design

Embed ethics into organizational processes.

### Ethics Theatre

Appearing ethical without meaningful action.

## AI Content Integrity

### Watermarking

Embed markers that identify AI-generated content.

### Content Provenance

Metadata that records where digital content originated and how it has
changed.
